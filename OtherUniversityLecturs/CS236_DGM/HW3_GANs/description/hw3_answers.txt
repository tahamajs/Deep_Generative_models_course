1     The Non-Saturating Generator Loss
In the standard Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) framework, the generator’s objective
is to produce samples that are indistinguishable from real data. A common issue with the
original minimax loss function for the generator, minG Ez∼pz (z) [log(1 − D(G(z)))], is that it can
lead to vanishing gradients, especially early in training when the discriminator can easily reject
generated samples (i.e., D(G(z)) ≈ 0). When this happens, the gradient of the loss with respect
to the generator’s parameters becomes very small, stalling the learning process.
    To address this, an alternative, widely-used objective is the ”non-saturating” generator loss.
Instead of minimizing the log-probability of the discriminator being correct, the generator aims
to maximize the log-probability of the discriminator being incorrect. This is equivalent to
minimizing the negative of that log-probability.

D∗ , given a fixed generator, G.

function V (D, G).

generator pθ is heavily penalized if it produces samples x for which pθ (x) > 0 but pdata (x) is very low.

can lead to high-quality samples, it also carries the risk of mode collapse, where the generator learns
to produce samples from only a subset of the modes present in pdata .


5     Problem 2.4: Comparing VAEs and GANs
Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) and GANs are two prominent families of deep generative models.
Their underlying objectives lead to distinct characteristics.

Problem 5.1. A VAE’s decoder is trained by maximizing the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO), which is
equivalent to minimizing the negative log-likelihood (NLL) of the data, −Ex∼pdata (x) [log pθ (x)].
    1. Show that minimizing this NLL is equivalent to minimizing KL (pdata (x) || pθ (x)).
    2. Compare this objective to the GAN generator objective from Problem 2.3. Are they learning the
       same thing?

Solution 5.1.1 (Part 1: NLL as KL Divergence). We want to relate the NLL to the ”forward” KL
divergence, KL (pdata || pθ ). By definition:
                                                                                

                            KL (pdata (x) || pθ (x)) = Ex∼pdata (x) log

Using the property of logarithms, log(A/B) = log A − log B:

                 KL (pdata (x) || pθ (x)) = Ex∼pdata (x) [log pdata (x)] − Ex∼pdata (x) [log pθ (x)]

Rearranging this equation to solve for the NLL term:

                −Ex∼pdata (x) [log pθ (x)] = KL (pdata (x) || pθ (x)) − Ex∼pdata (x) [log pdata (x)]

The term Ex∼pdata (x) [log pdata (x)] is the negative entropy of the true data distribution, −H(pdata (x)).
Since pdata (x) is fixed and does not depend on the model parameters θ, this term is a constant with
respect to the optimization of θ. Thus, we have:

This shows that minimizing the negative log-likelihood with respect to θ is equivalent to minimizing
KL (pdata (x) || pθ (x)).

Solution 5.1.2 (Part 2: Comparison). The question asks whether a VAE decoder and the GAN gener-
ator from Problem 2.3 are implicitly learning the same objective.
    • A VAE decoder, by maximizing the ELBO, effectively minimizes the forward KL divergence,
      KL (pdata || pθ ).
    • The GAN generator from Problem 2.3, when paired with an optimal discriminator, minimizes
      the reverse KL divergence, KL (pθ || pdata ).

These two objectives, KL (pdata || pθ ) and KL (pθ || pdata ), are not the same due to the asymmetry of
the KL divergence. They lead to different optimization behaviors:
     • Forward KL (KL (pdata || pθ ) - VAE-like): This divergence is minimized when pθ (x) is high
       wherever pdata (x) is high. If pθ (x) → 0 for some x where pdata (x) > 0, the term log(pdata (x)/pθ (x))
       becomes very large, heavily penalizing the model. To avoid this, pθ tends to ”cover” all regions
       where pdata has mass. This is known as mode-covering behavior. This can lead to blurry or
       averaged samples if the model is not expressive enough.
     • Reverse KL (KL (pθ || pdata ) - GAN-like): This divergence is minimized when pθ (x) is low
       wherever pdata (x) is low. If pθ (x) > 0 for some x where pdata (x) ≈ 0, the model is heavily
       penalized. To avoid this, pθ tends to only place mass in regions where pdata has significant mass.
       This is known as mode-seeking behavior, which can result in sharp samples but may lead to
       mode collapse.
Therefore, a VAE decoder and the specific GAN generator from Problem 2.3 are not implicitly learning
the same objective.

Synthesis and Concluding Thoughts
6     Summary of Key Learnings and Implications
This exploration of Generative Adversarial Networks has traversed from the foundational
mechanics of their adversarial training to the deeper theoretical underpinnings that govern
their behavior. Several key themes emerge:

    1. The Critical Role of Loss Functions: The choice of loss function is paramount. The
       initial minimax loss suffers from vanishing gradients, while the non-saturating loss
       provides a pragmatic solution. More advanced losses connect the GAN objective to specific
       statistical divergences.



                                                       7
  2. The Optimal Discriminator as a Theoretical Tool: The derivation of D∗ (x) = pdatap(x)+p
                                                                                       data (x)
                                                                                              θ (x)
     is a cornerstone of GAN theory. It allows the GAN objective to be re-expressed as
     minimizing a divergence and reveals that the optimal discriminator’s logits estimate
     the log-likelihood ratio log(pdata (x)/pθ (x)).
  3. GANs and Divergence Minimization: Many GAN formulations can be understood as methods
     for minimizing some form of statistical divergence between probability distributions.
     The specific divergence (e.g., Jensen-Shannon, forward KL, reverse KL, Wasserstein
     distance) dictates the learning dynamics.
  4. The Mode-Seeking vs. Mode-Covering Dichotomy: This distinction is crucial. Mode-seeking
     objectives (like reverse KL) tend to produce high-quality but potentially less diverse
     samples (risk of mode collapse). Mode-covering objectives (like forward KL) aim for
     diversity, sometimes at the expense of sample sharpness.
  5. Ongoing Evolution of GANs: The challenges of GAN training have spurred vast research
     into alternative loss functions (e.g., Wasserstein GAN [2, 4]), architectural innovations,
     and regularization techniques to make GANs more robust and capable.

   In conclusion, GANs represent a powerful and flexible framework for generative modeling.
Harnessing this power requires a careful understanding of their theoretical properties,
the nuances of their loss functions, and the practical challenges of their training. The
connections to information theory and statistical divergence measures offer a unifying perspective,
guiding the design of new and improved generative models.
