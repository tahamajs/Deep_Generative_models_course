\section{Comprehensive Background on Generative Adversarial Networks}

Before diving into the specific problems, let's establish a thorough understanding of GANs. Generative Adversarial Networks represent a groundbreaking approach to generative modeling that was introduced by Ian Goodfellow and his colleagues in 2014. Unlike traditional generative models that learn by directly maximizing likelihood (like VAEs or flow-based models), GANs employ an adversarial training paradigm.

\subsection{The Adversarial Game: A Conceptual Foundation}

At its core, a GAN can be understood as a two-player minimax game between:
- A \textbf{generator} $G_\theta$ that learns to produce fake samples that mimic real data
- A \textbf{discriminator} $D_\phi$ that learns to distinguish real samples from fake ones

The generator's goal is to fool the discriminator, while the discriminator's goal is to correctly identify real versus fake samples. This adversarial process leads to an equilibrium where the generator produces samples that are statistically indistinguishable from the true data distribution.

\subsection{Mathematical Formulation of GAN Training}

The original GAN objective is formulated as:
\[
\min_\theta \max_\phi V(D_\phi, G_\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}(x)} [\log D_\phi(x)] + \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p_z(z)} [\log(1 - D_\phi(G_\theta(z)))]
\]

This objective function has profound theoretical connections. When the discriminator is optimal for a fixed generator, the value function $V(D^*, G)$ becomes related to the Jensen-Shannon divergence between the data distribution and the generator's distribution.

\subsection{Practical Implementation Considerations}

In practice, several challenges arise:
1. \textbf{Mode collapse}: The generator may learn to produce only a limited variety of samples
2. \textbf{Training instability}: The adversarial dynamics can lead to oscillations or divergence
3. \textbf{Vanishing gradients}: Early in training, gradients for the generator may become too small

These challenges have spurred numerous innovations in GAN training, including the variants we'll explore in this assignment.

\subsection{Problem 1: Generative Adversarial Networks (15 points)}

\subsubsection{Minimax loss and vanishing gradients [5 points]}

The original GAN formulation establishes a minimax game between the generator and discriminator. The complete objective function is:

\[
\min_G \max_D V(D,G)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{\text{data}}}[\log D(x)] + \mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_z}[\log(1-D(G(z)))] .
\]

This formulation can be understood as maximizing the log-likelihood that the discriminator correctly classifies real samples while minimizing the log-likelihood that it incorrectly classifies fake samples as real.

\paragraph{The Vanishing Gradient Problem:} In practice, training the generator using the minimax objective leads to a significant challenge known as \textbf{vanishing gradients}. This occurs because the generator's loss function becomes saturated when the discriminator becomes too good at its job.

The generator's loss in the minimax formulation is:
\[
L_{\text{minimax}}^G (\theta; \phi) = \mathbb{E}_{z\sim N(0,I)} [\log(1 - \sigma(h_\phi(G_\theta(z))))].
\]

\textbf{Derivation of the gradient vanishing:} Let's derive why this leads to vanishing gradients. We start with the derivative of the generator loss with respect to its parameters θ:

\[
\frac{\partial L_{\text{minimax}}^G}{\partial \theta} = \mathbb{E}_{z\sim N(0,I)} \left[ \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} \log(1 - \sigma(h_\phi(G_\theta(z)))) \right].
\]

Using the chain rule:
\[
\frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} \log(1 - \sigma(h_\phi(G_\theta(z)))) = \frac{1}{1 - \sigma(h_\phi(G_\theta(z)))} \cdot (-\sigma'(h_\phi(G_\theta(z)))) \cdot \frac{\partial h_\phi(G_\theta(z))}{\partial \theta}.
\]

Since $\sigma'(x) = \sigma(x)(1 - \sigma(x))$, we have:
\[
-\sigma'(h) = -\sigma(h)(1 - \sigma(h))
\]

So the gradient becomes:
\[
\frac{\partial L_{\text{minimax}}^G}{\partial \theta} = \mathbb{E}_{z\sim N(0,I)} \left[ \frac{-\sigma(h)(1 - \sigma(h))}{1 - \sigma(h)} \cdot \frac{\partial h}{\partial \theta} \right] = \mathbb{E}_{z\sim N(0,I)} \left[ -\sigma(h) \cdot \frac{\partial h}{\partial \theta} \right].
\]

When the discriminator successfully identifies fake samples (i.e., $\sigma(h_\phi(G_\theta(z))) \approx 0$), the gradient $\frac{\partial L_{\text{minimax}}^G}{\partial \theta} \approx 0$, causing training to slow or stop.

\paragraph{Why This Is Problematic:} This vanishing gradient problem is particularly acute early in training when the generator produces obviously fake samples. The discriminator quickly learns to reject these samples with high confidence, leading to $\sigma(h) \approx 0$. This makes the gradient signal extremely weak, preventing the generator from learning effectively. The generator becomes "stuck" and cannot improve its samples.

\subsubsection{Non-saturating GAN implementation [10 points]}

To address the vanishing gradient problem, the non-saturating loss reformulates the generator's objective. Instead of minimizing the probability that the discriminator correctly identifies fakes, the generator maximizes the probability that the discriminator incorrectly classifies fakes as real.

The non-saturating generator loss is:
\[
L_G^{\text{non-saturating}}(\theta; \phi) = -\mathbb{E}_{z\sim N(0,I)} [\log \sigma(h_\phi(G_\theta(z)))].
\]

This is equivalent to maximizing $\mathbb{E}_{z} [\log \sigma(h_\phi(G_\theta(z)))]$, which encourages the generator to produce samples that the discriminator believes are real.

\textbf{Implementation Details:} The practical implementation uses Monte Carlo estimates over mini-batches. For a batch of size $m$:

\[
L_D(\phi; \theta) \approx -\frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m \log \sigma(h_\phi(x^{(i)})) - \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m \log(1 - \sigma(h_\phi(G_\theta(z^{(i)}))))
\]

\[
L_G^{\text{non-saturating}}(\theta; \phi) \approx -\frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m \log \sigma(h_\phi(G_\theta(z^{(i)})))
\]

where $x^{(i)} \sim p_{\text{data}}$, $z^{(i)} \sim N(0,I)$.

\textbf{Numerical Stability:} The implementation uses `torch.nn.functional.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits` or equivalent stable formulations to avoid numerical issues with very small probabilities.

\textbf{Training Dynamics:} The non-saturating loss provides much stronger gradients early in training. When $\sigma(h) \approx 0$ (discriminator confident about fakes), the gradient magnitude is approximately $1/\sigma(h)$, which is large and provides clear learning signal.

\textbf{Practical Considerations:}
- The discriminator loss remains unchanged from the original minimax formulation
- Training alternates between updating discriminator and generator
- Typically, the discriminator is updated multiple times per generator update to maintain balance
- The non-saturating formulation is the standard approach used in most modern GAN implementations

\subsection{Problem 2: Divergence Minimization (25 points)}

\subsubsection{Optimal discriminator characterization [10 points]}
Show that $L_D$ is minimized when $D_\phi = D^*$, where $D^*(x) = \frac{p_{\text{data}}(x)}{p_\theta(x) + p_{\text{data}}(x)}$.

\textbf{Derivation:} For fixed $x$, minimize $f(t) = -p_{\text{data}}(x) \log t - p_\theta(x) \log(1-t)$. Setting $f'(t) = 0$:
\[
-\frac{p_{\text{data}}(x)}{t} + \frac{p_\theta(x)}{1-t} = 0
\]
\[
p_\theta(x) t = p_{\text{data}}(x) (1-t)
\]
\[
t(p_\theta(x) + p_{\text{data}}(x)) = p_{\text{data}}(x)
\]
\[
t = \frac{p_{\text{data}}(x)}{p_\theta(x) + p_{\text{data}}(x)}
\]

Since $f$ is sum of strictly convex functions, this is the unique minimizer.

\subsubsection{Discriminator logits as log-likelihood ratios [5 points]}
When $D_\phi = D^* = \sigma(h_\phi(x))$, show that $h_\phi(x) = \log \frac{p_{\text{data}}(x)}{p_\theta(x)}$.

\textbf{Derivation:} $D^*(x) = \sigma(h_\phi(x)) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-h_\phi(x)}} = \frac{p_{\text{data}}(x)}{p_\theta(x) + p_{\text{data}}(x)}$
\[
1 + e^{-h_\phi(x)} = \frac{p_\theta(x) + p_{\text{data}}(x)}{p_{\text{data}}(x)} = 1 + \frac{p_\theta(x)}{p_{\text{data}}(x)}
\]
\[
e^{-h_\phi(x)} = \frac{p_\theta(x)}{p_{\text{data}}(x)}
\]
\[
-h_\phi(x) = \log \frac{p_\theta(x)}{p_{\text{data}}(x)}
\]
\[
h_\phi(x) = \log \frac{p_{\text{data}}(x)}{p_\theta(x)}
\]

\subsubsection{Specific generator loss and KL divergence [5 points]}
Consider $L_G(\theta; \phi) = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_\theta(x)} [\log(1 - D_\phi(x))] - \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_\theta(x)} [\log D_\phi(x)]$.
Show that if $D_\phi = D^*$, then $L_G(\theta; \phi) = KL(p_\theta(x)||p_{\text{data}}(x))$.

\textbf{Derivation:}
\[
L_G = \mathbb{E}_{p_\theta} [\log(1 - D^*)] - \mathbb{E}_{p_\theta} [\log D^*]
= \mathbb{E}_{p_\theta} \left[ \log \frac{1 - D^*}{D^*} \right]
= \mathbb{E}_{p_\theta} \left[ \log \frac{p_\theta}{p_{\text{data}}} \right]
= \mathbb{E}_{p_\theta} \left[ \log \frac{p_\theta(x)}{p_{\text{data}}(x)} \right] = KL(p_\theta||p_{\text{data}})
\]

\subsubsection{Comparing VAEs and GANs [5 points]}

This problem explores the fundamental differences between VAEs and GANs, revealing why they produce different types of generative models.

\textbf{Problem Statement:} Show that the negative log likelihood (NLL) minimized by VAEs can be written as $KL(p_{\text{data}}||p_\theta) + C$ where $C$ is constant w.r.t. $\theta$. Determine if VAEs and the GAN formulation from Problem 2.3 learn the same objective.

\textbf{VAE Objective Analysis:} VAEs maximize the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO):
\[
ELBO = \mathbb{E}_{q(z|x)} [\log p_\theta(x|z)] - KL(q(z|x)||p(z))
\]

The ELBO is a lower bound on the log-likelihood: $ELBO \leq \log p_\theta(x)$. Therefore, maximizing ELBO is equivalent to minimizing the negative ELBO, which upper bounds the NLL.

\textbf{Derivation:} The negative log likelihood is:
\[
-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{\text{data}}} [\log p_\theta(x)]
\]

We can decompose this using the KL divergence:
\[
KL(p_{\text{data}} || p_\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}} [\log p_{\text{data}}(x) - \log p_\theta(x)]
\]

\[
= \mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}} [\log p_{\text{data}}(x)] - \mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}} [\log p_\theta(x)]
\]

Therefore:
\[
-\mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}} [\log p_\theta(x)] = KL(p_{\text{data}} || p_\theta) - \mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}} [\log p_{\text{data}}(x)]
\]

Since $\mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}} [\log p_{\text{data}}(x)] = -H(p_{\text{data}})$ is constant with respect to $\theta$, VAEs effectively minimize the forward KL divergence $KL(p_{\text{data}} || p_\theta)$.

\textbf{Comparison with GANs:} The GAN formulation in Problem 2.3 minimizes $KL(p_\theta || p_{\text{data}})$, the reverse KL divergence.

\textbf{Key Differences:}

\textbf{Forward KL (VAEs):} $KL(p_{\text{data}} || p_\theta)$
- \textbf{Mode-covering behavior:} Penalizes the model when it assigns low probability to real data points ($p_\theta(x) \approx 0$ where $p_{\text{data}}(x) > 0$)
- \textbf{Objective:} Cover all regions where data exists
- \textbf{Result:} Diverse samples, potentially blurry/averaged if model capacity is insufficient

\textbf{Reverse KL (GANs):} $KL(p_\theta || p_{\text{data}})$
- \textbf{Mode-seeking behavior:} Penalizes the model when it generates samples not in the data distribution ($p_\theta(x) > 0$ where $p_{\text{data}}(x) \approx 0$)
- \textbf{Objective:} Focus on high-density regions of data
- \textbf{Result:} Sharp, high-quality samples, potential mode collapse

\textbf{Practical Implications:}
- VAEs excel at capturing the full diversity of the data distribution
- GANs excel at producing high-fidelity samples from learned modes
- The choice depends on whether diversity or quality is more important for the application

\subsection{Problem 3: Conditional GAN with Projection Discriminator (20 points)}

Conditional GANs extend the basic GAN framework to generate samples conditioned on auxiliary information, such as class labels. This allows for controlled generation where we can specify desired attributes of the output.

\subsubsection{Optimal conditional discriminator [10 points]}

This problem derives the optimal discriminator for conditional GANs under a specific probabilistic assumption about the data distribution.

\textbf{Problem Statement:} Suppose that when $(x, y) \sim p_{\text{data}}(x, y)$, there exists a feature mapping $\phi(x)$ such that both the conditional data distribution and the conditional generator distribution are mixtures of Gaussians. Specifically, assume:
\[
p_{\text{data}}(x|y) = \mathcal{N}(\phi(x)|\mu_y, I)
\]
\[
p_\theta(x|y) = \mathcal{N}(\phi(x)|\hat{\mu}_y, I)
\]

Show that under this assumption, the optimal discriminator logits have the form $h^*(x,y) = y^T (A \phi(x) + b)$.

\textbf{Derivation:} The optimal conditional discriminator maximizes:
\[
\max_D \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim p_{\text{data}}} [\log D(x,y)] + \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim p_\theta} [\log(1 - D(x,y))]
\]

For fixed $\phi(x)$, we need to find the optimal logits $h^*(x,y)$ such that $D(x,y) = \sigma(h^*(x,y))$.

The log-likelihood ratio is:
\[
\log \frac{p_{\text{data}}(x,y)}{p_\theta(x,y)} = \log \frac{p_{\text{data}}(x|y) p_{\text{data}}(y)}{p_\theta(x|y) p_\theta(y)}
\]

Assuming uniform priors over classes, the ratio becomes:
\[
\log \frac{p_{\text{data}}(x|y)}{p_\theta(x|y)} = \log \frac{\mathcal{N}(\phi(x)|\mu_y, I)}{\mathcal{N}(\phi(x)|\hat{\mu}_y, I)}
\]

For multivariate Gaussians with identity covariance:
\[
\log \frac{\mathcal{N}(\phi(x)|\mu_y, I)}{\mathcal{N}(\phi(x)|\hat{\mu}_y, I)} = -\frac{1}{2}||\phi(x)-\mu_y||^2 + \frac{1}{2}||\phi(x)-\hat{\mu}_y||^2
\]

\[
= -\frac{1}{2}(\phi(x)^T\phi(x) - 2\phi(x)^T\mu_y + \mu_y^T\mu_y) + \frac{1}{2}(\phi(x)^T\phi(x) - 2\phi(x)^T\hat{\mu}_y + \hat{\mu}_y^T\hat{\mu}_y)
\]

\[
= (\hat{\mu}_y - \mu_y)^T \phi(x) + \frac{1}{2}(||\hat{\mu}_y||^2 - ||\mu_y||^2)
\]

For one-hot encoded $y$, this gives:
\[
h^*(x,y) = y^T (A \phi(x) + b)
\]

where $A$ is the matrix with rows $\hat{\mu}_y - \mu_y$ and $b$ contains the bias terms.

\textbf{Interpretation:} This projection discriminator structure is highly efficient and interpretable. The discriminator learns to project the feature representation $\phi(x)$ into a space where different classes are linearly separable, with the one-hot label $y$ selecting the appropriate projection.

\subsubsection{Conditional GAN implementation [10 points]}

\textbf{Generator Architecture:} The conditional generator concatenates the one-hot encoded label $y$ to the latent vector $z$:
\[
G_\theta(z, y) = G_\theta([z; y])
\]

This allows the generator to condition its output on the desired class label.

\textbf{Discriminator Architecture:} The projection discriminator computes:
\[
D_\phi(x, y) = \sigma(y^T (A \phi(x) + b))
\]

where $\phi(x)$ is learned feature representation, $A$ and $b$ are learned parameters.

\textbf{Training:} The conditional GAN is trained with the same alternating optimization as standard GANs, but using labeled data $(x, y) \sim p_{\text{data}}(x, y)$.

\textbf{Advantages:}
- Enables controlled generation (e.g., "generate a shirt" vs "generate pants")
- Maintains class-specific features in generated samples
- Natural extension of unconditional GANs

\textbf{Implementation Details:}
- Use one-hot encoding for labels
- Ensure discriminator has access to both image and label
- Train on datasets with class labels (e.g., FashionMNIST, CIFAR-10)

\subsection{Problem 4: Wasserstein GAN (35 points)}

Wasserstein GANs address fundamental limitations of the original GAN formulation by optimizing the Wasserstein distance (also known as Earth Mover's distance) instead of the Jensen-Shannon divergence. This leads to more stable training and meaningful loss values.

\subsubsection{KL divergence for Gaussians [5 points]}

This problem illustrates why KL divergence can become problematic in high-dimensional, low-variance settings.

\textbf{Problem Statement:} Let $p_\theta(x) = \mathcal{N}(x|\theta, \epsilon^2)$ and $p_{\text{data}}(x) = \mathcal{N}(x|\theta_0, \epsilon^2)$ be univariate Gaussians with the same variance. Show that $KL(p_\theta||p_{\text{data}}) = \frac{(\theta - \theta_0)^2}{2\epsilon^2}$.

\textbf{Derivation:} The KL divergence for univariate Gaussians is:
\[
KL(\mathcal{N}(\mu_1, \sigma^2) || \mathcal{N}(\mu_2, \sigma^2)) = \frac{(\mu_1 - \mu_2)^2}{2\sigma^2}
\]

Therefore:
\[
KL(p_\theta || p_{\text{data}}) = \frac{(\theta - \theta_0)^2}{2\epsilon^2}
\]

\subsubsection{Limit behavior and instability [5 points]}

This problem shows why KL-based divergences can become unstable in certain regimes.

\textbf{Problem Statement:} Consider the limit $\epsilon \to 0$. What happens to $KL(p_\theta||p_{\text{data}})$ and its derivative with respect to $\theta$ when $\theta \neq \theta_0$?

\textbf{Analysis:} As $\epsilon \to 0$:
\[
KL(p_\theta || p_{\text{data}}) = \frac{(\theta - \theta_0)^2}{2\epsilon^2} \to \infty
\]

The derivative with respect to $\theta$:
\[
\frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} KL = \frac{2(\theta - \theta_0)}{2\epsilon^2} = \frac{\theta - \theta_0}{\epsilon^2} \to \infty
\]

\textbf{Implications:} In the limit of point masses (very narrow distributions), the KL divergence becomes infinite and its gradients explode. This causes extreme instability in GAN training, as the discriminator can become infinitely confident about distinguishing real from fake samples.

\subsubsection{Problems with unconstrained Wasserstein critic [5 points]}

Wasserstein distance provides a more stable alternative, but unconstrained optimization leads to degenerate solutions.

\textbf{Problem Statement:} Consider the limit $\epsilon \to 0$ where both distributions become point masses at $\theta$ and $\theta_0$. Show that the unconstrained Wasserstein critic objective has no optimal solution.

\textbf{Wasserstein Distance:} The Wasserstein-1 distance between distributions $P$ and $Q$ is:
\[
W_1(P, Q) = \sup_{||f||_L \leq 1} \mathbb{E}_P[f(x)] - \mathbb{E}_Q[f(x)]
\]

where the supremum is over 1-Lipschitz functions $f$.

\textbf{Problem Analysis:} For point masses at $\theta$ and $\theta_0$, the Wasserstein critic loss becomes:
\[
L_D = \mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}[f(x)] - \mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}}[f(x)] = f(\theta) - f(\theta_0)
\]

Without Lipschitz constraints, we can make this arbitrarily large by setting $f(\theta) \to \infty$ or $f(\theta_0) \to -\infty$. Therefore, no optimal critic exists for unconstrained optimization.

\subsubsection{Lipschitz-constrained critic [5 points]}

Constraining the critic to be 1-Lipschitz resolves the optimization issues.

\textbf{Problem Statement:} Restrict the critic to 1-Lipschitz functions (functions with derivative in [-1, 1]). What is the optimal critic for point masses at $\theta$ and $\theta_0$?

\textbf{Solution:} For 1-Lipschitz functions on the real line, the optimal critic between two points is:
\[
f^*(x) = c - |\theta - \theta_0|
\]

for $x$ between $\theta$ and $\theta_0$, where $c$ is chosen to satisfy the Lipschitz constraint.

The Wasserstein distance becomes $W_1 = |\theta - \theta_0|$.

\subsubsection{WGAN-GP implementation [15 points]}

WGAN-GP adds a gradient penalty to enforce the Lipschitz constraint approximately.

\textbf{WGAN-GP Objective:}
\[
L_D = \mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}[f(x)] - \mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}}[f(x)] + \lambda \mathbb{E}_{p_{\hat{x}}} [(||\nabla f(x)||_2 - 1)^2]
\]

where $p_{\hat{x}}$ is the distribution of samples interpolated between real and fake data:
\[
\hat{x} = \alpha x_{\text{real}} + (1 - \alpha) x_{\text{fake}}, \quad \alpha \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)
\]

\textbf{Implementation Details:}
1. Sample real batch $x_{\text{real}} \sim p_{\text{data}}$
2. Sample fake batch $x_{\text{fake}} = G(z), z \sim p_z$
3. Sample interpolation coefficients $\alpha \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)$
4. Create interpolated samples $\hat{x} = \alpha x_{\text{real}} + (1 - \alpha) x_{\text{fake}}$
5. Compute gradient penalty: $(||\nabla_\hat{x} f(\hat{x})||_2 - 1)^2$
6. Total critic loss: $\mathbb{E}[f(x_{\text{fake}})] - \mathbb{E}[f(x_{\text{real}})] + \lambda \mathbb{E}[(||\nabla f(\hat{x})||_2 - 1)^2]$

\textbf{Generator Loss:} $L_G = -\mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}[f(x)]$

\textbf{Advantages of WGAN-GP:}
- Stable training without careful hyperparameter tuning
- Meaningful loss values that correlate with sample quality
- No need for batch normalization or careful learning rate schedules
- Better gradient flow throughout training

\textbf{Implementation Considerations:}
- Use $\lambda = 10$ (common default)
- Compute gradients efficiently by calling `torch.autograd.grad` on the sum of critic outputs
- Apply gradient penalty to interpolated samples to enforce Lipschitz constraint locally

\subsection{Problem 5: Noise Contrastive Estimation (5 points)}

Noise Contrastive Estimation (NCE) is an alternative approach to learning energy-based models that avoids computing the partition function Z. This problem shows the connection between NCE and GAN discriminators.

\subsubsection{Optimal NCE parameters [5 points]}

\textbf{Problem Statement:} NCE optimizes the objective:
\[
\max_{\theta,Z} \mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}(x)} [\log \sigma(-E_\theta(x) - \log Z - \log p_{\text{noise}}(x))] + \mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{noise}}(x)} [\log(1 - \sigma(-E_\theta(x) - \log Z - \log p_{\text{noise}}(x)))]
\]

Show that the optimal parameters $(\theta^*, Z^*)$ satisfy $\frac{1}{Z^*} \exp(-E_{\theta^*}(x)) = p_{\text{data}}(x)$.

\textbf{Derivation:} At optimality, the NCE objective matches the optimal GAN discriminator objective. The optimal discriminator for distinguishing data from noise satisfies:
\[
\log \frac{p_{\text{data}}(x)}{p_{\text{noise}}(x)} = -E_{\theta^*}(x) - \log Z^* + C
\]

Exponentiating both sides gives the desired result.

\textbf{Connection to GANs:} NCE can be seen as a way to learn energy-based models using the same discriminator approach as GANs, but with a tractable noise distribution instead of a learned generator.

\textbf{Derivation:} Using the optimal discriminator result, the NCE objective leads to $E_{\theta^*}(x) + \log Z^* + \log p_{\text{noise}}(x) = \log \frac{p_{\text{data}}(x)}{p_{\text{noise}}(x)}$. Exponentiating gives the result.

\subsection{Architectures}
We replicate the exact layer choices used in the codebase (`src/codebase/network.py`). For clarity we restate them here:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Generator (dim_z = 64, for grayscale 28x28 images):

      \item Linear(512, 64*7*7) + BatchNorm + ReLU
      \item Reshape(64,7,7)

      \item PixelShuffle(2) followed by Conv2d(32//4, 1, k=3, p=1) (output logits scaled to [-1,1] with tanh or left as raw)

  \item Discriminator:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Conv2d(1,32,k=4,stride=2,p=1) + LeakyReLU(0.1)
      \item Conv2d(32,64,k=4,stride=2,p=1) + LeakyReLU(0.1)
      \item Flatten, Linear(64*7*7,512) + LeakyReLU(0.1)
      \item Linear(512,1) (logit output for standard GAN)
    \end{itemize}
  \item ConditionalGenerator / ConditionalDiscriminator: the conditional generator concatenates a one-hot label vector to the latent $z$ and otherwise follows the same upsampling pipeline. The conditional discriminator produces per-class logits and selects the logit corresponding to the given label (implementation detail: `gather` on the final linear output).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Comprehensive Implementation Details}

\subsubsection{Network Architecture Implementation}

\subsubsection{Non-Saturating GAN Loss Implementation}

The non-saturating loss addresses the fundamental vanishing gradient problem of the original GAN formulation.

\begin{verbatim}
def loss_nonsaturating(g, d, x_real, *, device):
    """
    Implements the non-saturating GAN loss from Goodfellow et al. (2014)
    Section 3.2. This formulation provides stronger gradients for G.

    Key insight: Instead of minimizing log(1-D(G(z))), maximize log D(G(z)).
    This prevents vanishing gradients when D becomes confident.

    Args:
        g: Generator network (maps z -> x_fake)
        d: Discriminator network (maps x -> logits)
        x_real: Real images [batch_size, 1, 28, 28]
        device: torch.device

    Returns:
        d_loss: BCE loss for discriminator training
        g_loss: Non-saturating loss for generator training
    """
    batch_size = x_real.shape[0]

    # Generate fake images from random latent vectors
    z = torch.randn(batch_size, g.dim_z, device=device)
    x_fake = g(z)

    # Discriminator forward passes
    d_real_logits = d(x_real)      # Should be high (close to +∞)
    d_fake_logits = d(x_fake.detach())  # Should be low (close to -∞)

    # Discriminator loss: BCE with targets [1, 0] for [real, fake]
    # E[log σ(d_real)] + E[log(1 - σ(d_fake))] = E[log σ(d_real)] + E[log σ(-d_fake)]
    d_loss = F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(
        d_real_logits, torch.ones_like(d_real_logits)  # Target: real → 1
    ) + F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(
        d_fake_logits, torch.zeros_like(d_fake_logits)  # Target: fake → 0
    )

    # Generator loss: Non-saturating formulation
    # Maximize E[log D(G(z))] = minimize -E[log σ(d_fake)]
    # Fresh forward pass to avoid autograd conflicts
    z_g = torch.randn(batch_size, g.dim_z, device=device)
    x_fake_g = g(z_g)
    d_fake_g_logits = d(x_fake_g)

    g_loss = F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(
        d_fake_g_logits, torch.ones_like(d_fake_g_logits)  # G wants D to classify as real
    )

    return d_loss, g_loss
\end{verbatim}

\textbf{Implementation Notes:}
- Uses `.detach()` on fake images during discriminator update to prevent G gradients
- Fresh latent sampling for G loss to avoid tensor reuse conflicts
- `binary_cross_entropy_with_logits` combines sigmoid + BCE for numerical stability

\subsubsection{Conditional GAN Loss Implementation}

Extends the basic GAN framework to handle class-conditional generation.

\begin{verbatim}
def conditional_loss_nonsaturating(g, d, x_real, y_real, *, device):
    """
    Conditional GAN loss where both G and D receive class labels.
    Enables controlled generation of specific classes.

    The key insight: G(z, y) and D(x, y) both condition on labels.
    During training, we use the same labels for fake generation.

    Args:
        g: Conditional generator G(z, y) -> x
        d: Conditional discriminator D(x, y) -> logits
        x_real: Real images [batch_size, 1, 28, 28]
        y_real: Class labels [batch_size]
        device: torch.device

    Returns:
        d_loss: Conditional discriminator BCE loss
        g_loss: Conditional generator non-saturating loss
    """
    batch_size = x_real.shape[0]

    # Generate fake images conditioned on real labels
    z = torch.randn(batch_size, g.dim_z, device=device)
    y_fake = y_real  # Use real labels for fake generation (standard practice)
    x_fake = g(z, y_fake)

    # Discriminator evaluates (image, label) pairs
    d_real_logits = d(x_real, y_real)  # Real image + correct label
    d_fake_logits = d(x_fake.detach(), y_fake)  # Fake image + its generation label

    # Same BCE loss structure as unconditional GAN
    d_loss = F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(
        d_real_logits, torch.ones_like(d_real_logits)
    ) + F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(
        d_fake_logits, torch.zeros_like(d_fake_logits)
    )

    # Generator loss with fresh samples
    z_g = torch.randn(batch_size, g.dim_z, device=device)
    x_fake_g = g(z_g, y_fake)
    d_fake_g_logits = d(x_fake_g, y_fake)

    g_loss = F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(
        d_fake_g_logits, torch.ones_like(d_fake_g_logits)
    )

    return d_loss, g_loss
\end{verbatim}

\textbf{Conditional Training Dynamics:}
- Generator learns class-specific generation patterns
- Discriminator learns to evaluate image-label consistency
- Enables applications like "generate image of class k"

\subsubsection{WGAN-GP Loss Implementation}

Implements the Wasserstein GAN with gradient penalty for stable training.

\begin{verbatim}
def loss_wasserstein_gp(g, d, x_real, *, device):
    """
    Wasserstein GAN with Gradient Penalty (WGAN-GP).
    Based on Gulrajani et al. "Improved Training of Wasserstein GANs".

    Key improvements over standard GAN:
    1. Uses Wasserstein distance instead of JS divergence
    2. Enforces 1-Lipschitz constraint via gradient penalty
    3. More stable training, meaningful loss values

    Args:
        g: Generator network
        d: Critic network (no sigmoid, outputs real values)
        x_real: Real images [batch_size, 1, 28, 28]
        device: torch.device

    Returns:
        d_loss: Critic loss with gradient penalty
        g_loss: Generator loss
    """
    batch_size = x_real.shape[0]

    # Generate fake images
    z = torch.randn(batch_size, g.dim_z, device=device)
    x_fake = g(z)

    # Critic outputs (unbounded real values, no sigmoid)
    d_real = d(x_real)
    d_fake = d(x_fake.detach())

    # Wasserstein critic loss: E[critic(real)] - E[critic(fake)]
    # (minimize negative of this)
    wasserstein_loss = torch.mean(d_real) - torch.mean(d_fake)

    # Gradient penalty for Lipschitz constraint enforcement
    # Sample random interpolation points between real and fake
    alpha = torch.rand(batch_size, 1, 1, 1, device=device)
    x_interpolate = alpha * x_real + (1 - alpha) * x_fake
    x_interpolate.requires_grad_(True)

    # Critic output on interpolated samples
    d_interpolate = d(x_interpolate)

    # Compute gradients w.r.t. interpolated inputs
    gradients = torch.autograd.grad(
        outputs=d_interpolate.sum(),  # Scalar for gradient computation
        inputs=x_interpolate,         # Differentiate w.r.t. interpolated points
        create_graph=True,            # Allow higher-order derivatives
        retain_graph=False
    )[0]

    # Compute L2 norm of gradients for each sample
    gradients = gradients.view(batch_size, -1)  # Flatten spatial dims
    grad_norms = gradients.norm(2, dim=1)       # L2 norm per sample

    # Gradient penalty: E[(||∇f(x)||_2 - 1)^2]
    gradient_penalty = ((grad_norms - 1) ** 2).mean()

    # Final critic loss
    lambda_gp = 10.0  # Standard value from WGAN-GP paper
    d_loss = -wasserstein_loss + lambda_gp * gradient_penalty

    # Generator loss: maximize E[critic(fake)] = minimize -E[critic(fake)]
    z_g = torch.randn(batch_size, g.dim_z, device=device)
    x_fake_g = g(z_g)
    d_fake_g = d(x_fake_g)
    g_loss = -torch.mean(d_fake_g)

    return d_loss, g_loss
\end{verbatim}

\textbf{WGAN-GP Implementation Details:}
- Critic outputs unconstrained real values (no sigmoid activation)
- Gradient penalty enforces approximate 1-Lipschitz constraint
- Interpolation sampling ensures penalty applies across the data manifold
- $\lambda = 10$ balances the Wasserstein and penalty terms

\textbf{Stability Advantages:}
- No vanishing gradients or mode collapse issues
- Loss values correlate with sample quality
- More predictable training dynamics than standard GANs

\section{Synthesis of Theoretical Insights}

\subsection{Fundamental GAN Theory}

The core insight of GANs lies in their formulation as a two-player minimax game:
\[
\min_G \max_D V(D, G) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}} [\log D(x)] + \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p_z} [\log(1 - D(G(z)))]
\]

At equilibrium, when $D$ is optimal for a fixed $G$, the value function relates to the Jensen-Shannon divergence between data and generator distributions. This theoretical foundation explains why GANs can learn to generate samples from complex, high-dimensional distributions.

\subsection{Optimal Discriminators and Statistical Divergences}

A key theoretical contribution is the characterization of optimal discriminators:

\[
D^*(x) = \frac{p_{\text{data}}(x)}{p_{\text{data}}(x) + p_G(x)}
\]

This leads to the discriminator's logits estimating the log-likelihood ratio $\log \frac{p_{\text{data}}(x)}{p_G(x)}$, connecting GANs to classical statistical decision theory.

Different generator loss functions correspond to different statistical divergences:
- **Non-saturating loss** → Jensen-Shannon divergence
- **Problem 2.3 formulation** → Reverse KL divergence
- **VAE objective** → Forward KL divergence

\subsection{Wasserstein GANs and Optimal Transport}

WGANs optimize the Wasserstein-1 distance (Earth Mover's distance) instead of statistical divergences. This provides:
- More stable training dynamics
- Meaningful loss values that correlate with sample quality
- Better theoretical convergence guarantees
- Reduced issues with vanishing gradients and mode collapse

The gradient penalty enforces the 1-Lipschitz constraint approximately, enabling stable optimization of the Wasserstein distance.
